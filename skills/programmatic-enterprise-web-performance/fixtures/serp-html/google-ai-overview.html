A cash offer on a house is a real estate purchase agreement where the buyer pays for the property outright using their own liquid funds, rather than taking out a mortgage loan from a bank or lender.
Despite the name, buyers rarely bring physical dollar bills. Instead, the money is transferred electronically via a wire transfer or cashier’s check at closing.
How a Cash Offer Works
  • No Financing Contingency: The deal does not depend on a lender approving a loan. This removes the risk of the transaction falling through due to a buyer's bad credit, lost job, or low appraisal.
  • Proof of Funds: Because there is no bank involved to vet the buyer, the buyer must provide
    proof of funds upfront. This usually includes bank statements, a brokerage account summary, or an official letter from a financial institution.
  • Faster Closing: Without lender underwriting or loan processing delays, a cash sale can close in as little as 1 to 2 weeks (compared to 30 to 60 days for a traditional mortgage).
Pros and Cons for Sellers
AdvantagesDisadvantages
High certainty: Zero risk of the buyer's mortgage getting denied.Potentially lower price: Cash buyers often expect a discount or negotiate a lower purchase price in exchange for speed and convenience.
Fast timeline: Can close in weeks, saving months of utility, tax, and mortgage payments.Narrower pool of buyers: Fewer people have enough liquid cash to buy a home outright.
As-is convenience: Cash buyers are frequently more willing to purchase homes with deferred maintenance or major repair needs.Fast move-out pressure: A rapid closing can leave the seller scrambling to pack and relocate.
Important Realities to Keep in Mind
  • Not Risk-Free: Even without a mortgage, cash deals can still fall through if the buyer backs out during the inspection period, or if title issues, property liens, or inheritance disputes emerge.
  • Still Have Closing Costs: While cash buyers skip lender-related mortgage fees, they are still responsible for other closing expenses, such as title insurance, escrow fees, and pre-paid property taxes.
  • Inspections Are Still Smart: Skipping a mortgage doesn't mean you should skip a home inspection. Many cash buyers still opt for an inspection to avoid hidden structural surprises.
